on faith

august 3rd, 2025 ~15 min read

"Did you convert to Islam?"

I get this question a lot, given I'm now married to a muslim woman. But the answer isn't a simple yes or no. What I hope to do with this essay is walk you through why that is.

One of the reasons I hesitate to give a clear answer is because I've always had a fear of being put in a box. One of the greatest gifts my parents gave me was the space to think for myself — to question, explore, and arrive at my own truths. Anytime I feel pushed into a rigid identity or expected to adopt one, I tend to resist.

So for me to say "yes" would feel like I'm inviting people to label me — to assume what I should or shouldn't do, believe, or become. And I've never operated well in that kind of framework.

I've actually been around Islam for most of my life. I spent 15 years living in Dubai. I learned to read Arabic. I had mostly Muslim friends. I experienced Ramadan firsthand — waking up to the call to prayer echoing through the streets, watching people fast, break bread, gather in prayer.

But despite all of that, I never really took the time to understand the why behind the practices. And truthfully, neither did most people around me. Islam, for many of my peers, was inherited — rituals passed down from parents, followed without reflection. Praying five times a day, fasting during Ramadan, avoiding alcohol — they were seen as obligations, not invitations.

In fact, a lot of the relationships I witnessed between people and their faith were built on fear. Islam was presented as a strict rulebook: follow it or face punishment. Obey, or risk hell. That version of religion never sat right with me.

So when I was told I'd need to convert to Islam in order to marry Manal, it threw me into a bit of a personal storm. Not because I was unwilling to explore — but because I refused to say "yes" to something I hadn't genuinely thought through.

📖 Qur'an 2:221 — Marriage to Polytheists (Mushrikeen)

Arabic:

وَلَا تُنكِحُوا الْمُشْرِكِينَ حَتَّىٰ يُؤْمِنُوا ۚ وَلَعَبْدٌ مُّؤْمِنٌ خَيْرٌ مِّن مُّشْرِكٍ وَلَوْ أَعْجَبَكُمْ ۗ

Translation:

"And do not marry polytheistic men [to your women] until they believe. A believing slave is better than a polytheist, even though he may please you."

It felt like I had two options: either reject Islam and risk creating tension within the family, or blindly accept it just to go through with the nikah — but in doing so, compromise my values of honesty, integrity, and authenticity.

So I chose a third path.

I decided to take the time to understand Islam from first principles — not through the lens of an imam, cultural dogma, or pressure, but by going directly to the text itself. To read the Quran with fresh eyes. To reflect deeply. And then, only if it resonated, to make an informed and intentional choice to "convert." (You'll see why I put "convert" in quotes by the end of this piece.)

Starting from first principles

One of the most valuable tools I learned as an engineer is the ability to think from first principles. It's a way of approaching any problem or complex topic by stripping it down to its most fundamental truths, then reasoning upward from there — not relying on assumptions or inherited ideas, but starting fresh.

That's exactly how I wanted to approach my exploration of Islam.

And this is where the beauty of AI — specifically ChatGPT — came in. Without it, my only real option would have been to learn through the guidance of an imam. But my concern with that approach is this: imams often interpret the Quran through traditional or cultural frameworks, and that — in my view — is one of the greatest challenges within the religion today. When the Quran is filtered through dogma or rigid tradition, I believe it becomes harder to access its true essence. You end up engaging more with someone else's opinion about God, rather than forming a direct relationship with the divine yourself. That risks turning faith into a pursuit of approval, rather than understanding.

That said, I'm not naive about the limitations of AI either. ChatGPT, while powerful, has its flaws — it can fabricate information or reinforce whatever argument you present, which runs the risk of pulling you away from the Quran's actual message.

So here's how I approached my discovery process:

  1. For all my questions, I asked ChatGPT to build arguments strictly based on what's in the Quran, citing verses directly — no outside commentary or assumptions.
  2. When I was forming my own reflections or philosophical takes, I had it critique my reasoning, helping me stay grounded in the actual text, not just personal projections.

Now that you have the context — here's how things unfolded.

The fundamentals of Islam

📖 Surah Al-Baqarah (2:177)

Arabic:

لَّيْسَ الْبِرَّ أَن تُوَلُّوا وُجُوهَكُمْ قِبَلَ الْمَشْرِقِ وَالْمَغْرِبِ، وَلَٰكِنَّ الْبِرَّ مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَالْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْكِتَابِ وَالنَّبِيِّينَ، وَآتَى الْمَالَ عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ ذَوِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَابْنَ السَّبِيلِ وَالسَّائِلِينَ وَفِي الرِّقَابِ، وَأَقَامَ الصَّلَاةَ وَآتَى الزَّكَاةَ، وَالْمُوفُونَ بِعَهْدِهِمْ إِذَا عَاهَدُوا، وَالصَّابِرِينَ فِي الْبَأْسَاءِ وَالضَّرَّاءِ وَحِينَ الْبَأْسِ، أُولَٰئِكَ الَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا، وَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُتَّقُونَ

Translation:

Righteousness is not in turning your faces toward the east or the west, but true righteousness is in one who believes in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets. It is in those who give their wealth, despite loving it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask for help, and for freeing those in bondage. It is in those who establish prayer and give zakah, who fulfill their promises when they make them, and who are patient in times of poverty, hardship, and during moments of struggle. These are the ones who have been true, and these are the ones who are mindful of God."

To me, the purpose of religion is simple: to serve as a guide for becoming the highest version of yourself. And the Quran, in many ways, feels like a battle-tested guide — one that has endured for centuries and continues to help people live with purpose, alignment, and depth.

My intention here is to investigate each core belief in detail — to clarify whether and how my understanding of life aligns with what the Quran teaches. From there, I'll outline the actions I choose to take in accordance with Arkān al-Islām (the Five Pillars of Islam), as a way to live out those beliefs in practice.

Belief in Allah

📖 Surah Al-Hashr (59:22–24)

هُوَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِى لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ عَٰلِمُ ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَـٰدَةِ ۖ هُوَ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

He is Allah, other than whom there is no deity, the Knower of the unseen and the witnessed. He is the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful.

Shahid Baba is someone I respect deeply, not just because he's Manal's dad, but because of the way he approaches life, spirit, faith, and mysticism. He carries a sense of curiosity, but not blind faith. He asks questions, seeks coherence, and genuinely tries to understand. That's why a conversation I had with him 1 am in the morning last year, before our wedding day, completely shifted how I view God, Islam, and religion.

From that conversation, and through my own lens as a man of science, here's how I've come to understand God and Allah:

There can only be one Creator, just like there was only one Big Bang. That moment wasn't just an explosion — it was a beginning. From it, the universe has been unfolding according to consistent laws, guided by ever-increasing entropy. And behind that unfolding — the force that drives it, shapes it, and gives it structure — is what I call God (or ethnolinguistically called Allah)

Not a man in the sky. Not a human-like deity. But the intentional force behind all creation. The one who chose to initiate it all, and continues to guide its evolution with purpose.

"With purpose" — that's the key.

Entropy, to me, isn't a random drift into disorder. It's a divine unfolding. It doesn't mean every outcome is pre-written, but that each moment builds on the next with structure and direction. You see it in how all humans have eyes, arms, and a nose. How our brains share the same architecture. How the laws of physics never slip. That kind of coherence doesn't come from chaos. There's too much design, too much symmetry too much intention for it to just be a coincidence.

And so, for this reason, I very much align with the belief in Allah. Not as a figure with a face or form — but as the force of creation itself.

Belief in the Day of Judgement (and the afterlife)

اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۚ لَيَجْمَعَنَّكُمْ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ ۚ وَمَنْ أَصْدَقُ مِنَ اللَّهِ حَدِيثًا

"Allah — there is no deity except Him. He will surely assemble you for [account on] the Day of Resurrection, about which there is no doubt. And who is more truthful than Allah in statement?"

— Surah An-Nisa (4:87)

The belief in the Day of Judgment has always been a bit contentious for me. For most of my life, I held the view that once we die, that's it — the story ends there. I also found it strange when people shaped their entire lives around the promise of paradise in the afterlife. Does that really make sense? As someone who thinks like an engineer and a scientist, it never quite landed for me.

But over the years, that's started to shift. I've begun to understand it differently.

I like to think of the Day of Judgment not as a man in the sky accounting for all the good and bad deeds I've done, but more as the moment I reflect back on my life — like being on my deathbed. In that moment, I'd be asking myself: did my actions, intentions, and energy align with truth, generosity, and coherence? It's not about religious scorekeeping. It's the final, honest accounting of whether I lived in integrity with my highest values.

Just like I see entropy as something that divinely unfolds — moment to moment — I believe that the state of my consciousness at the end matters. If I've lived in alignment, and my final moments are filled with clarity, peace, and lightness, I imagine the energy of that consciousness will carry forward in some form. Maybe it takes the shape of another human being. Maybe it dissolves into the soil. But the energetic frequency of it will be high — and I trust that it will ripple through creation in a meaningful way.

Belief in Angels and Devils

لَا يَعْصُونَ اللَّهَ مَا أَمَرَهُمْ وَيَفْعَلُونَ مَا يُؤْمَرُونَ

"They (angels) do not disobey Allah in what He commands them but do what they are commanded."

— Surah At-Tahrim (66:6)

After spending more time with the Quran, I've come to see that it's written in a form that's both poetic and precise. Some verses speak in metaphor and symbolism, inviting deep reflection and layered interpretation. Others are meant to be taken literally, offering clear guidance for how to live. Like any powerful text, the Quran holds both — direct instruction and metaphorical insight.

I interpret the symbolism behind the angels and devils as metaphors for higher and lower forces.

Compassion, truth, presence, generosity — these are angelic (higher) forces. Greed, ego, cruelty, and deception — these are devilish (lower) ones.

Lower forces live within all of us — it's part of being human. But the daily test is: what choices are you making? Those who follow the path of the devil (lower forces) often carry hate, anger, and bitterness in their hearts. To me, that's a kind of hell on earth — where your inner world is consumed by resentment and negativity. That fire isn't just metaphorical — it burns from the inside out.

Just like there are lower forces, there are higher ones too — forces that pull you toward generosity, truth, patience, and love. When you choose to act from those places, something in you softens. You feel lighter, more expansive. To me, that's heaven on earth. It's the peace that comes when your inner world is in harmony with the deeper order of reality. That clarity, that stillness — it's not some distant reward, it's something you can taste now, in moments of real alignment.

Belief in the Books, Muhammad as the final messenger, in Qadar (Divine Decree)

مَّا كَانَ مُحَمَّدٌ أَبَآ أَحَدٍۢ مِّن رِّجَالِكُمْ وَلَـٰكِن رَّسُولَ ٱللَّهِ وَخَاتَمَ ٱلنَّبِيِّۦنَ ۗ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمًۭا

"Muhammad is not the father of any of your men, but he is the Messenger of Allah and the seal of the prophets. And Allah is ever, of all things, Knowing."

— Surah Al-Ahzab (33:40)

One of the hardest things for me when I first started exploring Islam was getting past the noise. The press, the rules, the cultural baggage — so much of it felt harsh, especially around how women were treated, or the way obedience seemed to be valued over inner understanding. I couldn't resonate with a version of religion that felt like it was more about control than truth.

What I came to realize is that the Quran itself isn't the problem — the interpretations are. Over time, ruling classes, governments, clergy, and religious institutions used the Quran to enforce obedience, not expand awareness. The message was mediated through imams and scholars who acted like gatekeepers, often bringing ego, politics, and fear into something that was meant to be pure and elevating. When access to divine truth is controlled like that, it's not surprising that lower forces creep in.

But when I started actually reading the Quran for myself, things shifted. I believe the Quran is a divine book — not because someone told me to, but because when I sat with it, it made sense. It speaks in the language of principles, reflection, consequence, and alignment. It doesn't feel like mythology — it feels like a framework for living in truth. The Quran to me reads like poetry. And like any deep poetry, it's meant to be sat with, questioned, and interpreted through lived experience.

The Prophet Muhammad wasn't just the conveyor of the message — he lived it. That's what makes him the final messenger to me. He wasn't perfect, but he was deeply aligned. He embodied the values in the Quran: patience, clarity, humility, and integrity. I'm not inspired by him out of obligation. I follow his example because his teachings are practical, grounded, and elevate the quality of life. They create better human beings.

And all of it — the book, the messenger, the unfolding of my own life — rests within a larger structure. That's how I understand Qadar. It doesn't mean everything is fixed or predetermined like a script. It means everything exists within divine structure — including my choices. The laws of physics, the unfolding of entropy, the emergence of consciousness, and even the circumstances of my life — none of that feels random. It feels like intentional design.

So I live as though my choices are both recorded and remembered — not because I fear punishment, but because they contribute to the architecture of something much bigger. A divine system. A moral order. And I get to shape it — decision by decision, intention by intention.

As I mentioned earlier, the core intention of Islam — to me — is not to practice the religion for the sake of ritual alone. It's to use its beliefs and practices as a guide for living a rich, fulfilling life, grounded in truth, purpose, and the highest version of consciousness I can embody.

Growing up, I saw many of my Muslim friends follow the rituals to the letter — praying, fasting, avoiding alcohol — but rarely questioning why those practices existed in the first place. Maybe, in some ways, doing the practice — even without understanding — is still better than not doing it at all. But for me, that's not enough.

I want to examine every practice deeply — not to find loopholes, but to understand the essence of it. And then, to lay out clear, intentional actions for how I will engage with each of the Five Pillars of Islam — in a way that is aligned with my values and awareness.

The 5 Pillars

It's worth noting that the Quran doesn't explicitly list out "five pillars." These practices were identified and organized by scholars over time — drawn from repeated messages throughout the Quran and the life of the Prophet. They're not a checklist, but a framework distilled from deeper principles.

Salah (Prayer)

أَقِمِ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ لِذِكْرِى

"Establish prayer to remember Me."

— Surah Taha (20:14)

Growing up, I remember going to temples as a kid. My parents would ask me to look at the deity, fold my hands, and pray. But I never really understood what that meant. Do I just close my eyes? Do I ask for things? Recite a mantra? And if so, why?

These rituals followed me into high school — especially since I went to a school grounded in Hindu tradition. And as most people know, Hinduism comes with a ritual for everything: for money, for strength, for health, for knowledge. On one hand, even when I didn't understand the meaning behind it, the act of prayer sometimes made me feel good. And maybe that feeling itself is meaningful enough.

But over time, I began to realize that prayer wasn't really about ritual. It was about remembrance — a moment to reconnect with the divine force that created me. A reminder that I'm not in control, and I don't need to be.

To pray is to surrender — not out of weakness, but out of trust. It's how I release control over the future, soften anxiety, and return to that deep knowing that God — the force behind all creation — is holding it all together, including me.

The Qur'an says prayer is for remembering Allah. I take that literally. For me, prayer is presence. It's when I come back to my breath, notice sensations in my body, feel whatever tension or worry is there — and then let it go. Not because I have the answer, but because I don't have to carry it alone.

That's why my prayer happens during morning meditation, during my evening wind-down, and in the quiet rituals before bed. But more than that — I try to make it a constant presence. In the small, everyday things: folding laundry, listening to birds, walking down the street, feeling the sun on my skin. Each of those is an opportunity to realign with Allah — not from obligation, but from gratitude.

That is my Salah.

Zakat (Charity)

خُذْ مِنْ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ صَدَقَةًۭ تُطَهِّرُهُمْ وَتُزَكِّيهِم بِهَا وَصَلِّ عَلَيْهِمْ ۖ إِنَّ صَلَوٰتَكَ سَكَنٌۭ لَّهُمْ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

"Take, [O Muhammad], from their wealth a charity by which you purify them and cause them increase, and invoke [Allah's blessings] upon them. Indeed, your invocations are reassurance for them. And Allah is Hearing and Knowing."

— Surah At-Tawbah (9:103)

A popular Hindu tradition when visiting temples is to drop some money into the donation box before praying. I always found that odd as a kid. I remember doing it before every big moment — an exam, a health scare, or when I really wanted something.

Looking back, I realize I built a transactional relationship with God. The mindset was: "I'm giving you money — now please give me what I want." But over time, I started to understand how giving with expectation corrupts the essence of generosity.

Charity, to me, isn't a transaction — it's a way of being. It's about living with generosity in every interaction, decision, and moment. I don't give because I think it will look good on some divine scoreboard. I give because that's just who I am. That's who I'm becoming.

If I give and the person turns out to be ungrateful or doesn't use it "the right way" — I don't regret giving. Because my intention wasn't about them. It was about me showing up fully, honestly, and with sincerity.

When a kid from a country that can't afford TKS reaches out for help, I give them my time — not out of obligation, but because I genuinely want them to succeed.

When someone in my dance community wants to grow, I support them — whether they can pay me or not.

When a coworker's plate is full, I step in — even if it's not "my job" — because I want them to win.

I give $100 a month to a Zen temple that's helped me connect with the divine — because that space healed me, and I want it to stay open for others.

When a friend has a dream that aligns with something higher, I go above and beyond to help make it real — not because it's "fair," but because it's the right thing to do.

Charity is generosity without keeping score.

It's how I offer up time, energy, money, or attention — with no strings attached.

And I trust that the force behind creation, Allah, sees that.

Not to reward me, but because it matters. Because it leaves an imprint on the world.

Sawm (Fasting)

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الصِّيَامُ كَمَا كُتِبَ عَلَى الَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَتَّقُونَ

"O you who have believed, fasting is prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, that you may become mindful (of God)."

— Surah Al-Baqarah (2:183)

Fasting, for me, is a way to deepen my spiritual practice. It's not just about giving up food — it's about what happens inside me when I'm faced with hunger, fatigue, and discomfort. That's when my emotions show up raw. That's when I'm challenged to surrender more deeply to God, to trust more, to let go. In those moments, I'm not just skipping meals — I'm rewiring my relationship with control, with craving, with presence. That's the real fast.

It also reminds me of the people who go through this every day — not out of choice, but because of circumstance. Fasting builds compassion. It brings gratitude. And it pushes me to give more to others, not just think about myself.

That said, I also know how my body works. I burn around 2900 calories a day. If I fast the traditional way, I won't meet my nutritional needs, and over time, that's going to hurt my health. And I don't believe the force that created me so precisely — with this metabolism, this physiology — would want me to break it in the name of a rule. That's not devotion, that's denial.

So I choose to fast in a way that aligns with how I'm built. I extend my prayer. I become more intentional about generosity. I focus on feeding others — whether that's literally giving meals or just nourishing the people around me with time, support, or presence. That, to me, is still fasting. It's sacrifice, it's stillness, it's devotion — just expressed in a way that's true to who I am.

Hajj (Pilgrimage)

وَإِذْ جَعَلْنَا ٱلْبَيْتَ مَثَابَةًۭ لِّلنَّاسِ وَأَمْنًۭا ۖ وَٱتَّخِذُوا۟ مِن مَّقَامِ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ مُصَلًّۭى ۖ وَعَهِدْنَآ إِلَىٰٓ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ وَإِسْمَـٰعِيلَ أَن طَهِّرَا بَيْتِىَ لِلطَّآئِفِينَ وَٱلْعَـٰكِفِينَ وَٱلرُّكَّعِ ٱلسُّجُودِ

"And [mention] when We made the House a place of return for the people and [a place of] security. And take, [O believers], from the standing place of Abraham a place of prayer. And We charged Abraham and Ishmael, [saying], 'Purify My House for those who perform Tawaf and those who are staying [there] for worship and those who bow and prostrate [in prayer].'"

— Surah Al-Baqarah (2:125)

Hajj is beautiful. I hope to go one day. When I understand the essence of Hajj — I see the pause, the purification, the remembrance — that's something I try to live every year.

I believe the intention behind Hajj is powerful — it's not just a pilgrimage, it's a full reset. A break from the noise of everyday life to remember what actually matters. It's a chance to step out of your routine, strip yourself of ego, status, and distraction — and come back to truth. That reset, I think, is necessary for everyone. But I don't believe it only needs to happen once in a lifetime or only in Mecca.

For me, I recreate that intention through an annual Vipassana retreat every December. Ten days of silence, meditation, and service. Ten days without my phone, without identity, where I sit with myself and remember who I am beneath it all. It's where I come back to the stillness that created me. Where I reconnect with the divine by doing nothing but being. And in doing so, I also help others reconnect with that same stillness in themselves.

Shahada (Declaration of Faith)

So now comes the question everyone wants an answer to: "Did you convert?"

"Did you take the Shahada?"

Traditionally, the process of conversion involves saying one sentence out loud:

"There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah."

Preferably in front of an imam, to make it "official."

But what I found interesting is that this process — the Shahada — doesn't actually appear in the Quran in that form. Nowhere does the Quran lay out a ritual declaration. What it does emphasize, over and over, is belief. Conviction. Alignment. Saying the words doesn't mean much if your life doesn't reflect it.

I actually had a 90-minute conversation with an imam, thinking I'd need some kind of approval — some stamp — to make it real. And I'm grateful for that conversation. He asked good questions, made sure I was approaching this from a place of sincerity. But in the end, it didn't feel right for me to declare my relationship with the divine through another human being. That connection is too personal. Too sacred. So I chose not to say the Shahada formally — not because I'm afraid to, but because I already live it.

To me, belief isn't about recitation — it's about embodiment.

I believe in the oneness of God. I believe in the role Muhammad played in delivering divine guidance.

But more than that, I try to live by those principles.

That, to me, is what truly matters.
